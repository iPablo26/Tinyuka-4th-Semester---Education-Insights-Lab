**AltSchool Africa · Data Science · Semester 4 · Individual Capstone (Part A)**

> **Primary question:** After accounting for socioeconomic conditions, are per-pupil expenditure and broadband access still associated with mathematics proficiency in US K-12 schools?

---

## 1. Project brief

### The problem
Schools differ widely in math outcomes, and decision-makers need to know which differences are linked to things they can influence (funding, technology access) and which mostly reflect underlying poverty. A raw comparison can mislead: richer areas tend to have *both* more spending and better broadband *and* higher scores, so a simple correlation may credit the wrong factor.

### The stakeholder
A **state education department or education foundation** deciding where to direct funding and support across K-12 schools.

### Why it matters
If spending or broadband genuinely relate to outcomes *beyond* poverty, they are levers a funder can act on. If they mostly stand in for poverty, money spent on them alone may not shift outcomes. Telling these apart avoids misdirected investment.

### Primary research question
After accounting for socioeconomic conditions (free/reduced lunch), are per-pupil expenditure and broadband access still associated with math proficiency across US K-12 schools?

### Supporting questions
1. How strongly is poverty associated with math proficiency, and how much of it do spending and broadband absorb?
2. Does the spending association differ between low- and high-poverty schools?
3. Do Title I schools differ in math proficiency, and is that just the poverty gradient?
4. Is the broadband gap a rural-urban gap or a poverty gap?
5. How well can school characteristics *predict* math proficiency, and which features does a model rely on?

### Assumptions
- Each row is one institution; `institution_id` is a reliable unique key.
- `percent_free_reduced_lunch` is a reasonable school-level proxy for socioeconomic disadvantage.
- Missing SAT/ACT/AP-IB/postsecondary values are **structural** (not applicable at that school level), not random (tested in notebook 02).
- Proficiency scores at their maxima (reading 98, math 96) are genuine ceilings (censored values), not errors.
- The 98% local funding seen in every Private Non-Profit and Private Religious school is a **fixed funding mix** tied to school control (0% federal, 2% state, 98% local), not a cap.

### Limitations
- **Observational, cross-sectional data:** associations only, never causation.
- **School-level data:** results do not necessarily describe individual students.
- **Unmeasured factors:** instruction quality, curriculum, prior achievement and leadership are not in the data.
- **Measurement:** the exact definitions of "proficiency" and "broadband access" are not documented; broadband is access only (not quality or use).
- **Clustering:** schools in the same district/state are not fully independent. A state-clustered check gave very similar results; district-level clustering could not be tested.
- **Dataset provenance:** the source, collection year and exact variable definitions of the dataset are not documented, and it may be partly synthetic. The findings are patterns *in this dataset*, not verified facts about US schools.
- **Scope:** 358 colleges/universities were excluded as a different population; results apply to K-12 schools.

## 2. Key findings

| Finding | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Poverty is the dominant factor** | r = -0.59 with math proficiency; adding it lifts R² from 0.08 to 0.37; the poorest third of schools average about 21 points below the least poor third |
| **Spending remains associated after accounting for poverty** | 1.25 -> 0.69 points per extra $1,000 (95% CI 0.59 to 0.79); stable across 11 alternative specifications (plus the baseline) |
| **Broadband does not, once poverty is accounted for** | Raw r = 0.45, adjusted coefficient 0.03 points (95% CI -0.09 to 0.16); R² gain about 0.00004 |
| **The "digital divide" here is a poverty divide** | Poverty explains 57% of broadband variation; rural-urban locale explains 9% |
| **Prediction is modest, and simple models match complex ones** | Headline R² about 0.36 (repeated cross-validation); the single seed-42 held-out test split scored 0.31 (95% CI 0.25 to 0.36); linear ≈ random forest ≈ gradient boosting; on that test set about half of schools were predicted within 10 points |

All of this describes **association, not cause**. Full evidence, caveats and recommendations are in notebooks 04 to 06.

## 5. Methods at a glance

- **Cleaning:** structural missingness kept as NaN (not imputed); graduation rate set to missing for elementary and middle schools; the private fixed-funding pattern, proficiency ceilings and ratio mismatches flagged; K-12 scope selected with evidence.
- **EDA:** distributions, LOWESS scatterplots, correlation heatmap, and stratified views that hold poverty roughly constant.
- **Statistics:** Fisher-z correlation CIs; Welch ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis, Tukey and eta²; chi-square with Cramér's V; nested OLS with HC3 robust errors, robust Wald F-tests, VIF, bootstrap CIs, an interaction test, diagnostics and 11 alternative-specification robustness checks (including state-clustered errors).
- **Machine learning (Option B, regression):** baseline, linear, ridge, random forest, gradient boosting; tuned by cross-validation; model chosen by the one-standard-error rule; evaluated on a held-out test set (MAE, RMSE, R²) with a bootstrap interval; interpreted with coefficients, permutation importance and partial dependence; ablation by feature group.
